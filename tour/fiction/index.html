Built for a producer before coffee

The first Ariel was built for one person: Tara’s husband, a development producer for fifteen years. She watched his mornings and kept a list of what made him cranky before the coffee had even worked.

  • Forty tabs, three newsletters and a Google Alert that only ever finds the stories everyone else has already found.
  • The perfect book, optioned last Tuesday by someone who happened to read the trade press a day earlier.
  • A week chasing an agent to learn the screen rights went in March.
  • A strong idea with no why now so the commissioner says “interesting, but why this year?”
  • The brilliant foreign-language story nobody in the room could read in time.
So Tara built him an Ariel

Something that reads everything overnight and leaves only what matters on the doormat.

It worked well enough that other producers asked for one. Today every Ariel is still built the same way: around one person’s slate, with Tara on the other end of the email.

Tara Button is a published author with an MA in Writing for Young People, a former BBC writer and subeditor, and the founder of BuyMeOnce. She knows what a good story looks like, and how many of them never reach the people who could make them.

Named after the spirit in The Tempest who hears everything and reports back. “This will I tell my master.”

Built around you

Nothing in your Ariel is generic. These are the parts we shape with you on the call, and that you can keep changing from your dashboard.

Your shelves, in your words

Literary, crime and thriller, historical, debuts, translated fiction, or a shelf that only you would name. Your dashboard is sorted the way you think about books.

Your lens

A few sentences, in the first person, that every book is read against: the kind of world, the scale of story, what you would never make.

An example lens“Character-led literary and historical fiction with a strong world, contemporary or period, that could run to six hours. Debuts welcome. No fantasy.”

Your sources

Deal announcements, review pages, prize lists and agents’ rights guides, plus the literary Substack or podcast you already trust.

Before publication

Deals are flagged when they are announced, often a year or more before the book reaches a shop.

Your look

Your dashboard and email are designed around your company’s own identity, not a template.

Your rhythm and your team

Many drama producers prefer once a week. Add colleagues so the whole development team sees the same list.

Boston Globe · Wikipedia

  • What I Want My Words to Do to You (2003, PBS POV). Documentary about a prison writing group in which Judith Clark appears.
  • One Battle After Another (Paul Thomas Anderson, 2025). The Boston Globe’s reviewer compares its radical-mother storyline to the novel’s. Overlap to know about, not the same story.
  • The Tempest (Donmar Warehouse, 2016). Judith Clark inspired the role of Hannah, played by Harriet Walter. Ariel would have liked that one.
  • It is fiction drawn from a real case with real victims: the guard Peter Paige and police officers Waverly Brown and Edward O’Grady were killed. Families and the police community will have a view.
  • The Judith Clark story itself has been told in press and documentary, so the novel’s inner, daughter’s-eye view is what is new.
  • The title will need thinking about for search and marketing.
  • The author’s literary agent, for film and TV rights. Not named on the publishers’ pages, so this is the first thing we would confirm for you.
  • Sceptre / Farrar, Straus and Giroux rights departments can route an enquiry if the agent is slow to surface.

Every brief gives you

  • Where it stands: screen, rights, access, production, timing
  • A fair read of strength and difficulty
  • What is already on screen, and what overlaps
  • Who to call first, with a route to reach them

No brief will

  • Pitch your film back to you
  • Say rights are available unless the rights holder says so
  • Make a claim without a link to its source
  • Stamp a story “pursue” or “pass”. You decide.

The questions senior producers ask

Straight answers, before you apply.

Who sees my slate?

You, the colleagues you add, and Tara. Your lens, notes and saved stories are private to your Ariel. They are never shown to agents or to other producers.

Will someone else get the same story?

Public news is public, so another producer could see the same article. What differs is what your Ariel picks, and why. Agents’ titles go only to producers whose brief they fit, and an introduction happens only when you ask for it.

How do I know the rights are real?

We only mark a title Rights available when the agent or their published rights list says so, with the date it was confirmed. Anything else says “unverified, confirm with the agent”. We recheck titles that sit in a feed for a while.

Can I check everything?

Yes. Every story links to the original, and every claim in a brief links to its source. If we can’t source it, we don’t say it.
